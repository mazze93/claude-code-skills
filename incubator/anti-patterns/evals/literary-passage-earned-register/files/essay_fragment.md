# The Weight of Unanswered Mail

My father kept letters. Not the way an archivist keeps letters — in
boxes, labeled, chronological — but the way a man keeps things that
he has not yet decided whether to answer. In a drawer, on the
kitchen table, in the inside pocket of a coat he wore twice a year
and left on a chair between seasons. A letter he had read and set
down was a letter he was keeping company with. A letter he had
answered was a letter he had let go of. The unanswered ones
accumulated weight the way rooms accumulate dust — invisibly, until
someone opens the door after an absence.

When he died, the letters were in three places. A folder marked
*correspondence* in the filing cabinet, which held exactly nothing
of correspondence, only receipts and tax forms, and which I think
he kept labeled that way because the word pleased him. A wooden box
on the shelf above his desk, which held letters from his mother
written between 1968 and 1974, every single one answered, every
single one returned to him after her death in a package tied with
twine by a cousin we never met. And a drawer in the kitchen table —
the one between the one with the mismatched silverware and the one
with the dish towels — which held, when I opened it, forty-three
letters, all unanswered, the oldest from 1979 and the most recent
from the week before he fell.

I counted them before I read any of them. I think I needed to know
how much there was before I knew what any of it said. Forty-three.
Not so many that a lifetime of correspondence could not have been
caught up on, but too many to have been an oversight. He had, at
some point, begun to keep the letters he could not answer rather
than throw them away.

What I thought about, standing at the kitchen table with the drawer
open, was that there must have been a reason he did not answer them
and a reason he did not throw them away, and that those two reasons
might have been the same reason or they might have been two
different reasons. The letter was both a thing that had asked
something of him and a thing he had chosen to keep near. He had
not responded; he had also not discarded. The drawer was the shape
of his indecision, forty-three folded sheets of it, lined up like
teeth.

I read them, eventually. Most were not what I expected. Several
were from the same sister-in-law who wrote once a year every
November asking the same two questions she never got answers to,
and whose last letter, dated three weeks before he died, closed
with *I hope this reaches you*. Several were from people I did not
know, and from whom I could not tell, from the letters themselves,
what the relationship had been. One was from my mother, dated
1991, written from a hotel in a city I had not known she had been
to, and I will not describe it here. One was from me — I had
forgotten entirely that I had written it — sent in the first week
of my first job, when I was twenty-three and he was fifty-nine and
I had believed, with a confidence I now find touching and terrible
in equal measure, that there would be time for the conversation
the letter asked for.

There was, and there wasn't.

---

What I want to say about the drawer — and what I have been circling
around for the eight months since I emptied it — is that the
unanswered letters were not a record of his failure. They were a
record of a specific form of honesty: he did not answer letters he
could not answer well. The sister-in-law's questions had answers
that would have cost more than he was willing to say in writing to
a woman he loved and did not trust. My mother's letter had an
answer that he had not yet lived into. My own had an answer that
he, I think, had been waiting to give in person, and had not
arranged the in-person in time.

The drawer was not negligence. It was a practice. He practiced,
for forty-six years, the discipline of not answering the question
he could not answer honestly. In most frames this would be a
failure of character. In the frame I have been working toward, it
is something else — it is the weight of what he would not
counterfeit, kept in a drawer so he would remember it was still
his to answer when he was ready.

I do not know whether this is what he was doing. He left no note,
the letters are the only record, and I am reading them from the
outside of a life I knew less of than I thought I did. But I know
what I am going to do with them. I am going to answer them. Not
all of them, and not all at once, and not as him — as me, writing
from his address, saying what I think he would have said if he had
had the time he thought he had. The letters that already had their
answer lived in him and died with him will stay in the drawer. The
ones where the answer can be reconstructed from what he kept of
them — the margins annotated, the dates circled, the sentences
underlined — those I can take a run at.

---

Perhaps there is no answer at all. Perhaps the asking is the thing,
and my father's drawer was less a record of unanswered questions
than a shrine to the act of being asked. Perhaps the letters were
not addressed to him any longer by the time he opened the drawer
in the kitchen each evening to look at them, but to a person he
was practicing becoming — someone who would, eventually, be the
sort of man the letters assumed he was. Perhaps answering them
now, on his behalf, is the arrogance of the living toward the
dead, and I should leave the drawer the way he left it.

I do not think so. But I am naming the thought because the thought
is the kind of thought that keeps a person from writing the first
reply, and I know this because I am the person who has not
written the first reply yet.
