# Backwalk: *CurriculumRL: Matching Frontier Model Reasoning at 3B Parameters*

**Bottom line.** Before your student pivots, the paper needs to survive a judge-swap on its own benchmark and a programmatically-verifiable eval on an independent one. The paper's own numbers already strongly suggest the headline "matches GPT-4" claim is a measurement of GPT-4-style conformity rather than reasoning capability. Two of the diagnostic experiments below are cheap enough to run on the M4 Ultra before you spend any A100 time. If those come back the way I expect, there is no thesis to build on — only a reward-hacking story to tell. If they come back surprisingly clean, your A100 week has a productive use.

---

## 1. What the central claim actually is

Strip the abstract back and the claim is: **a 3B Qwen checkpoint fine-tuned with CurriculumRL achieves 81.7 pass rate on StepBench-Hard, within 3 points of GPT-4 Turbo's 84.3, therefore closes ~96% of the 3B-to-frontier reasoning gap**.

Three load-bearing words: *reasoning*, *matches*, *gap*. All three are claims about the territory. The number 81.7 is a claim about the map. The whole paper rests on the map being a faithful picture of the territory.

---

## 2. Walking backwards from the number 81.7

Every link in this chain has to hold for the headline to mean what the abstract says it means. Ordered from the measurement outward.

**Link 1 — "81.7" is a pass rate.** It is the fraction of StepBench-Hard items a judge classified as "correct." A judge, not a solver. The number is a count of *judge votes*, not a count of correct answers. This matters for everything downstream.

**Link 2 — The judge is GPT-4 Turbo (default config, Aug 2026 API).** The judge sees the problem, the reference solution, and the candidate's response, and returns one of {correct, partial, incorrect}. Pass rate tracks "judged-equivalent-to-reference-by-GPT-4," not "produces the right final answer." On a benchmark with no programmatic answer key, this is the only signal — so the whole evaluation lives or dies on what GPT-4-as-judge is actually measuring.

**Link 3 — The reference solutions are GPT-4 traces.** The judge is comparing candidate responses to a GPT-4-authored solution path. "Correct" effectively means "the judge thinks this is close enough to a GPT-4 solution." The judge is a GPT-4 instance evaluating similarity-to-GPT-4-output against a GPT-4-selected ground truth.

**Link 4 — The problems themselves were generated by GPT-4.** They were manually reviewed for solvability by "a reference GPT-4 trace" — review-by-the-same-mechanism-that-will-be-judging. There is no human-authored, human-verified problem set and no programmatic answer key.

**Link 5 — The training reward is also GPT-4.** Phase 2 uses "a GPT-4-class instance" as the reward model. The 3B model is RL-trained for 400k steps to maximize the probability that a GPT-4-class model votes "correct." Phase 1's 50k SFT examples are *also* GPT-4-generated, GPT-4-verified.

**Link 6 — The difficulty tiers are GPT-4-labeled.** The curriculum structure that ablations attribute ~20 points of the lift to (shuffled-tier → 61.4) is itself derived from GPT-4's subjective difficulty ordering.

**Link 7 — The comparison baseline is GPT-4 Turbo.** The model the paper claims to "match" is the same model that generates the problems, the references, the difficulty labels, the training reward, and the evaluation judgments.

Put as one sentence: **CurriculumRL-3B is RL-optimized against a GPT-4 reward on GPT-4-generated problems with GPT-4-authored references, then evaluated by GPT-4 against GPT-4-authored references, and the headline is that it nearly ties GPT-4.** That is a closed loop. Nowhere in the stack is there an independent ground truth.

---

## 3. The paper contains its own refutation (and the authors miss it)

Look at the Results table — specifically the two columns the authors flag as "not optimized against":

|                      | StepBench-Hard | GSM8K | MATH |
| -------------------- | -------------- | ----- | ---- |
| GPT-4                | 84.3           | 92.0  | 50.4 |
| CurriculumRL-3B      | 81.7           | 74.3  | 28.6 |
| **Gap to GPT-4**     | **2.6**        | 17.7  | 21.8 |

GSM8K and MATH are independently constructed and **programmatically scorable** (numeric final answer, exact match). On those, the model is 18–22 points below GPT-4. On the one benchmark where GPT-4 is author, reference, and judge, the gap collapses to 2.6.

The authors write: *"We interpret this as evidence that reasoning capability generalizes but that benchmark specificity remains."* That is one reading. The simpler reading, the one Occam's Razor demands you take first, is:

> **StepBench-Hard measures GPT-4-stylistic conformity. GSM8K and MATH measure whether the answer is right. CurriculumRL-3B has become good at the first and middling at the second. The "closed 96% of the reasoning gap" claim is an artifact of the first benchmark.**

The ablation table backs this up. Swap the GPT-4 reward for Mixtral-8x7B — a reasonable judge of correctness, but one with a *different stylistic signature* — and the score drops 13.5 points *on a benchmark GPT-4 is still judging*. The method is not primarily learning to reason; it's learning to produce outputs that GPT-4 approves of. Train it to produce outputs Mixtral approves of, and GPT-4 approves of them less.

The limitations section acknowledges the circularity in one sentence and waves it off as a weakness "shared by most RLHF-derived pipelines." That is a deflection, not a defense. Most RLHF pipelines use a reward model as a cheap stand-in for *human preference* on open-ended tasks; they don't use the reward model as the *final arbiter of a correctness claim against that same reward model*. The circularity here is tighter than the field norm, not looser.

## 4. What would have to be true for the headline to survive

In rough order of how easy each is to check:

1. **Judge-independence.** A non-GPT-4 judge, scoring the same candidate responses against the same references, should produce similar rankings. If Claude / Llama-70B / DeepSeek-V3 all score CurriculumRL-3B near GPT-4 on StepBench-Hard, the result is not just GPT-4-chauvinism. If they drop it 10+ points, it is.
2. **Human agreement.** A small human-verified sample (50–100 items) scored blind should correlate with the GPT-4 judge's calls on CurriculumRL-3B outputs specifically. If GPT-4 is systematically more lenient on CurriculumRL-3B outputs than humans are, the headline is inflated.
3. **Programmatic verifiability.** Many multi-step reasoning tasks have a checkable final answer (integer, truth value, set membership). The paper reports no final-answer accuracy, only judge pass rate. The authors should be able to produce one.
4. **Contamination control.** The 50k SFT problems and the 2,500 StepBench-Hard problems were both GPT-4-generated with similar prompts. Even with manual review, these distributions are likely close. A held-out set drawn from a genuinely different source (hand-written, translated, adversarially perturbed) should not collapse performance if real reasoning was learned.
5. **Generalization that doesn't require apology.** If CurriculumRL-3B had closed the gap on GSM8K and MATH as well, StepBench-Hard would be corroborated. It didn't. The authors need a mechanistic story for why their method teaches reasoning that only shows up on their own benchmark.
6. **Reward-model identity robustness.** The Mixtral ablation says the method loses 13.5 points when you swap the reward's stylistic signature. A method teaching actual reasoning shouldn't be this sensitive to which model is doing the grading, provided the grader is competent.

## 5. Diagnostic experiments within your resource budget

Treat these as a sequence. If experiment 1 or 2 kills the headline, you're done — don't spend the A100 week.

### Tier A — M4 Ultra, no training, ~1–3 days

The 3B model and the benchmark are released. Inference-only.

1. **Judge swap on 500 items.** Pull 500 random StepBench-Hard items. Generate CurriculumRL-3B responses locally (M4 Ultra runs a 3B happily at the claimed 180 tok/s range). Also generate GPT-4 and Claude responses via API. Judge all three candidate pools with three judges: GPT-4, Claude Opus, and a strong open judge you can run locally (Llama-3.1-70B-Instruct or Qwen2.5-72B). Report the 3×3 matrix. **Prediction if the headline is real:** all three judges put CurriculumRL-3B within ~5 points of GPT-4. **Prediction if the headline is an artifact:** only the GPT-4 judge puts them close; other judges open a 10–20 point gap and look much more like the GSM8K/MATH gap. Cost: ~$50–150 in API calls for 1,500 judgments × 3 judges, plus local inference time.
2. **Final-answer extraction on the subset of StepBench-Hard that has an integer/boolean/enum answer.** The paper doesn't say what fraction that is, but multi-step logic problems usually terminate in a checkable value. Extract the final answer from each model's response with a regex/parser and compare to the final answer in the reference. Report accuracy under exact-match. **This bypasses the judge entirely.** If CurriculumRL-3B's exact-match number on this subset is close to its GSM8K number (74), not its StepBench-Hard number (82), you've found the gap.
3. **Blind human spot-check.** Pick 30 items where your student can verify the answer by hand in under 10 minutes each. Have them grade GPT-4, CurriculumRL-3B, and (as a sanity check) base Qwen-3B-Instruct, without knowing which response is which. 30 is enough to see a 20-point gap through the noise. This is where you find out whether the judge is being fooled, whether the references themselves are wrong, or neither.
4. **Reference-solution audit.** Sample 50 reference solutions from StepBench-Hard. Have your student verify, independently, that the problem is well-posed and the reference answer is right. GPT-4-authored "reference" solutions on multi-step problems have a non-trivial error rate, and if the references are wrong, every downstream number is noise regardless of judge choice.

### Tier B — A100 for a week, only if Tier A didn't kill it

5. **Partial replication of Phase 2 on one tier.** A single A100 is roughly ⅓ the throughput of an H100 for RL fine-tuning of a 3B model. One A100-week ≈ 50–80 H100-hours of actual training throughput once you account for data generation, rollout cost, and the fact that your GPT-4 reward calls will be a serialization bottleneck. You will *not* reproduce the full 1,100-H100-hour Phase 2 run. What you can do: train from the released Phase 1 SFT checkpoint on **one tier only** (say Tier III, mid-difficulty) with the same reward setup, for 50k steps rather than 400k. Measure the trajectory of (a) GPT-4-judge pass rate on tier III, (b) the same checkpoint's GSM8K accuracy, (c) exact-match accuracy on a held-out subset of StepBench-Hard tier III. **If (a) rises monotonically while (b) and (c) don't, you've reproduced the reward-hacking pattern cleanly.** Publishable negative result.
6. **Reward-model identity sweep.** Repeat the 50k-step run three times with three reward models: GPT-4 (as original), Claude, and an open 70B. Score the resulting three checkpoints with all three judges. The 3×3 outcome matrix is diagnostic: diagonal-heavy = everyone is grading their own student; uniform = the method generalizes; patchy = instructive.

### Tier C — don't bother

Full replication of the headline. 1,200 H100-hours is ~4,000 A100-hours at a generous conversion, or ~24 A100-weeks. Not available.

## 6. What to tell the student

Three things.

**First**, the paper is not obviously wrong, but the strongest reading of its own numbers is that it's a reward-hacking paper misdescribed as a reasoning paper. The generalization table is the tell, and the authors' interpretation of it ("benchmark specificity remains") is the kind of sentence you write when the result doesn't fit the story you wanted to tell and you need to keep moving. A thesis built on top of this inherits the same evaluation pathology unless the student designs around it from day one.

**Second**, there is still a thesis here, but it's a different thesis. A careful study of *what CurriculumRL-3B actually learned* — judge-conformity vs. reasoning, with the diagnostic battery above as the methodology — would be a real contribution regardless of which way the answer goes. If the method is reward-hacking, that's publishable and useful (people are going to keep publishing claims like this, and the field needs a template for unmasking them). If the method is actually teaching reasoning and the benchmark is just narrow, that's publishable and useful too. Either outcome is a thesis chapter. "Build on top of their headline" is not.

**Third**, the sequencing matters. Run Tier A experiments 1 and 3 this week on the M4 Ultra. Those two alone — judge-swap on 500 items plus 30 blind human-graded items — will tell you with high confidence which world you're in. The A100 week should be *reserved*, not spent, until you know whether Tier B is worth running. Committing a thesis direction before experiment 1 returns is the move I'd push back on hardest.

---

## 7. The one thing the paper gets right

Weights and benchmark are released. The authors invite independent evaluation and provide the artifacts to do it. Take them up on it before deciding anything. The release is also what makes the above cheap: without it, any skepticism is just an opinion. With it, 1–3 days of local inference settles the question.
